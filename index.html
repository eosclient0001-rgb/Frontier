Great Hammerhead Shark Anatomy & Mechanics

1. Cephalofoil Hydrodynamics & Sensory Wing

The broad, T-shaped hammer head (cephalofoil) measures 23–27% of total body length in Sphyrna mokarran. Unlike other hammerheads, the Great Hammerhead features a nearly straight anterior margin with a shallow central indentation. The cephalofoil provides hydrodynamic lift, dynamic yaw stability, and houses hundreds of Ampullae of Lorenzini electroreceptive pores on its ventral surface to detect hidden benthic stingrays.

2. Kinetic Palatoquadrate Upper Jaw Protrusion

Sharks possess hyostylic jaw suspension. The upper jaw (palatoquadrate) is not fused to the chondrocranium. During a feeding strike, the lower jaw (Meckel's cartilage) depresses down, and the palatoquadrate detaches and protrudes forward and downward, projecting the razor-sharp triangular serrated teeth outside the snout profile. After the clamp, violent lateral head thrashing saws through prey tissue.

3. Procedural Turning & High Dorsal Banking

Hammerheads exhibit extraordinary lateral flexibility. In procedural turns, the vertebral column bends into an organic spline curve while the shark banks (rolls) up to 25°–45° into the turn. Adult Great Hammerheads frequently swim on a tilt, using their massive falcate 1st dorsal fin as a hydrofoil to generate lateral lift and turn "on a dime".

4. Heterocercal Caudal Fin & Dermal Denticles

The tail features a monumental upper lobe with a subterminal notch and a stiff lower hypochordal lobe. The skin is covered with microscopic placoid scales (dermal denticles) featuring longitudinal riblets that reduce drag and create an anisotropic velvety sheen with pristine countershading (slate dorsum to white belly).
